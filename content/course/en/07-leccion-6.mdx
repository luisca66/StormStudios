In previous lessons, you explored intervals, scales, and the internal structure of fifth chords (triads). Now, we take a fundamental step toward polyphony: learning how to distribute the notes of a chord across the four parts of the **harmonic vocal quartet** (soprano, alto, tenor, and bass). In this lesson, we focus exclusively on building and voicing isolated chords; we will not yet cover melodic motion or voice leading between chords, which we will study later.

## The Voices of the Quartet and Their Notation

The traditional vocal quartet consists of two female voices (**soprano** and **alto**) and two male voices (**tenor** and **bass**).

Historically in this method, exercises were written on a two-stave piano system using opposing stems: soprano and alto shared the treble clef (soprano stems up, alto stems down), while tenor and bass shared the bass clef (tenor stems up, bass stems down). On our platform, using **Storm Sequencer** in SATB mode, each of the four voices has its own independent staff, providing an unobstructed and intuitive view of every vocal line.

## Voice Separation and Voice Crossing

The overall texture and resonance of the quartet depend on the distance between adjacent voices. To maintain harmonic cohesion and prevent acoustic voids, we enforce the following **maximum separations**:

  <thead>
    <tr>
      <th>Adjacent Voice Interval</th>
      <th>Maximum Permissible Separation</th>
    </tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr>
      <td>Soprano – Alto</td>
      <td>A <strong>12th</strong> (compound fifth)</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Alto – Tenor</td>
      <td>A <strong>10th</strong> (compound third)</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Tenor – Bass</td>
      <td>A <strong>15th</strong> (double octave)</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Why can the bass be up to two octaves from the tenor? We want the three upper voices as compact as possible; the further the bass is from the rest, however, the more robust the chord sounds, because the low note generates more overtones on which the other chord tones settle.

Additionally, **voice crossings are not permitted**: soprano must always sing above alto, alto above tenor, and tenor above bass. Crossing parts disrupts vocal timbre and blurs the harmonic hierarchy.

## Doublings, Triplings, and Omissions

A fifth chord consists of three notes (root, third, and fifth), but the vocal quartet has four voices. Arranging a triad in four parts therefore requires doubling one of its factors or omitting one.

The rules for the four chord types are:

1. **The Leading Tone Rule**: you may double any chord factor **except the leading tone**. In a major scale, the leading tone is the **VII degree**. Across diatonic triads, the leading tone appears as the 3rd of V, the root of VII, and the 5th of III. The leading tone tends to resolve to the tonic; doubling it would put that resolution in two voices at once.
2. **Major and minor chords**:
   - *Complete*: containing root, third, and fifth, with one note doubled. The preferred doublings are the **root** or the **fifth**. Doubling the third is technically permissible, but it tends to sound somewhat harsh or heavy.
   - *Incomplete*: the **fifth may be omitted**. Never omit the root (which anchors the acoustic foundation) or the third (which defines the major or minor mode).
   - When omitting the fifth, you may double both the root and third (two roots and two thirds), or triple the root (three roots and one third). Tripling the third is very rare due to its acoustic harshness.
3. **Diminished and augmented chords**:
   - **Always complete**: never omit any factor, as the altered fifth is essential to defining the quality of the chord.
   - You may double any note except the leading tone (in the diminished triad on scale degree VII, double the third or the fifth; in the augmented triad on degree III of harmonic minor, double the root or the third).

## Chord States: Root Position and Inversions

The **state** of a chord is determined exclusively by which factor is placed in the lowest voice (**the bass**):

- **Root position**: when the root of the chord is in the bass.
- **First inversion (figured 6/3)**: when the third of the chord is in the bass.
- **Second inversion (figured 6/4)**: when the fifth of the chord is in the bass.

The figures **6/3** and **6/4** originate from historical figured bass notation and represent the intervals formed by the upper voices above the bass note:

- In first inversion, with the third in the bass, the remaining notes form a third and a sixth above it (figured as **6/3**, commonly abbreviated as 6).
- In second inversion, with the fifth in the bass, the upper notes form a fourth and a sixth above the bass (figured as **6/4**).

## Melodic Position

The **melodic position** describes which chord factor is placed in the highest voice (**the soprano**):

- **Melodic position of the 8th**: when the soprano sings the root of the chord.
- **Melodic position of the 3rd**: when the soprano sings the third of the chord.
- **Melodic position of the 5th**: when the soprano sings the fifth of the chord.

The melodic position determines the color and prominent pitch at the top of the chord, directly shaping the brightness of the soprano line.

## Internal Disposition: Close and Open Voicing

The **internal disposition** (or voicing) considers the spacing among the **three upper voices** (soprano, alto, and tenor), without regard to the distance to the bass:

- **Close disposition**: when no other chord tone fits either between soprano and alto or between alto and tenor.
- **Open disposition**: when a chord tone does fit within one of those pairs.

Close disposition concentrates vocal energy within a narrow band, whereas open disposition distributes the pitches across a wider span, giving the texture more breadth and air.

## Assignment for the Virtual Teacher

Now it is time to practice building and voicing chords inside the sequencer.

Write an exercise of **7 fifth chords (triads)**, one on each scale degree of a major key:

1. **Choose a major key** (whichever you prefer, such as C major, G major, F major, or E-flat major).
2. **Build all 7 degrees** of that scale (I, II, III, IV, V, VI, and VII). You may arrange them in any order you choose.
3. For each chord, freely choose its **state** (root position, 6/3, or 6/4), its **melodic position** (8th, 3rd, or 5th), and its **internal disposition** (close or open). We recommend varying these combinations across the seven measures to explore different voicings and sonorities.
4. Each chord must occupy a full 4/4 measure as a **whole note** across all four simultaneous voices.
5. Work in **Storm Sequencer** in SATB mode and export your file as MIDI to submit it for analysis.

### What the Virtual Teacher Checks

- **Key and degree identification**: recognizes your chosen scale and confirms that all 7 degrees are present, with no omitted or duplicated degrees.
- **Vocal ranges**: verifies that no note exceeds the permissible boundaries for soprano (C4–G5), alto (G3–D5), tenor (B2–G4), and bass (F2–B3).
- **Maximum voice separations**: ensures that intervals do not exceed a 12th between soprano and alto, a 10th between alto and tenor, or a 15th between tenor and bass.
- **Voice crossings**: confirms there are zero voice crossings throughout the exercise.
- **Doublings, triplings, and omissions**: checks compliance with complete and incomplete chord rules, and strictly verifies that the **leading tone is never doubled**.
- **Musical spelling**: verifies that accidentals and pitch spellings correctly match your chosen key.
- **Analytical report**: for each chord, the Virtual Teacher will detail in its output report the harmonic degree, state, melodic position, and internal disposition used.
