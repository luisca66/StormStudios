Before writing in four voices we need a common vocabulary: what the degrees are called, what each one does, what a chord is, and where the idea of **tonality** comes from. This lesson is explanatory only; it has no assignment for the Virtual Teacher.

## Degree names

Each scale degree has its own name. The names are arranged around the tonic: the **dominant** is a 5th above and the **subdominant** a 5th below; the **mediant** is a 3rd above and the **submediant** a 3rd below.

<div className="lesson-figure">
  <img src="/images/curso/leccion-5/grados-en.svg" alt="C major scale with the name of each degree: tonic, supertonic, mediant, subdominant, dominant, submediant and leading tone" />
</div>

The 7th degree is called the **leading tone** when it lies a semitone below the tonic, as in the major and harmonic minor scales. When it lies a whole tone below, as in natural minor, it is called the **subtonic**.

**Harmony** is the part of musical technique that studies both the intervallic structure of chords and the laws by which they are connected.

## The harmonies and the harmonic series

Rather than a single harmony, we are interested in **"the harmonies"**: those of each period, genre, style or composer. The harmonic series explains how new intervals were accepted over time. Each stage of history incorporated the next harmonics in the series.

## Chords of the basic scales

If we take each degree of the basic scales as the root of a fifth chord, we only get four chord types: **major**, **minor**, **diminished** and **augmented**. A chord is major or minor because of its third, and augmented or diminished because of its fifth. You built all of them in Lesson 4.

## Tonal regions

The chords of a scale are grouped into three **tonal regions**: **subdominant**, **tonic** and **dominant**.

<div className="lesson-figure">
  <img src="/images/curso/leccion-5/regiones-tonales-en.svg" alt="C major chords arranged in thirds: the subdominant region groups II, IV and VI; the tonic, VI, I and III; the dominant, III, V and VII" />
</div>

- Each region is represented by a **principal chord** that gives it its name (IV, I and V), and by two **secondary** chords a 3rd above and below it.
- The modal chords **III** and **VI** belong to two regions: VI to the subdominant and the tonic; III to the tonic and the dominant.
- Chords of the same region can **substitute** for each other without changing the harmonic sense of a passage.

## Tonality

**Tonality** is the set of relationships, functions and hierarchies that all the sounds of our system adopt around one of them, chosen beforehand as the **tonal center**: the **tonic**. To modulate is to replace one tonal center with another.

The altered (or unaltered) notes of a passage show its key. If we order the keys by the order in which new accidentals appear, we find a fixed relationship of **perfect 5ths**, both between the keys and between the accidentals:

<div className="lesson-figure">
  <img src="/images/curso/leccion-5/orden-alteraciones-en.svg" alt="Key signatures with seven sharps and seven flats showing the order in which accidentals appear" />
</div>

Each key signature serves two keys: a major key and its **relative minor**, a minor 3rd below. The circle of fifths gathers the **15 major and 15 minor keys**. At the bottom, three positions have two enharmonic names (B/C♭, F♯/G♭ and C♯/D♭) with different key signatures.

<div className="lesson-figure">
  <img src="/images/curso/leccion-5/circulo-quintas-en.svg" alt="Circle of fifths with the 15 major keys, their relative minors and their key signatures" />
</div>

Going clockwise, each key is a perfect 5th above the previous one and adds a sharp (or drops a flat). Going counterclockwise, each key is a perfect 5th below and adds a flat.
