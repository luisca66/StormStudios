Yesterday I told you that Lesson 6 was under construction. Today it is finished: [Lesson 6](/en/harmony-course/07-lesson-6-harmonic-vocal-quartet) of the [Harmony Course](/en/harmony-course) is published in English and Spanish, with its video.

## Four voices, each on its own staff

This lesson takes us into four-part writing. We learn to distribute the notes of a chord among **soprano, alto, tenor and bass**, the harmonic vocal quartet. We don't move the voices from one chord to the next yet; first we learn to build each chord well.

In the original course we wrote the quartet on a piano score, with opposite stems. In the sequencer's SATB mode each voice has its own staff, which makes it much easier to read.

## What you will learn

- **Vocal ranges**: the lowest and highest note of each voice.
- **The maximum spacing** between neighboring voices, and why they must not cross.
- **Doublings and omissions** in major, minor, diminished and augmented chords, and why the leading tone is never doubled.
- **Chord states**: root position, 6/3 and 6/4.
- **Melodic position**: the octave, the third or the fifth, depending on the note the soprano sings.
- **Internal spacing**: close or open.

## The assignment, reviewed in four parts

The assignment is to write the seven chords of a major scale, in the key of your choice and in any order, one chord per measure, in four parts. For the first time, the [Virtual Teacher](/en/virtual-teacher) reviews a four-part exercise: it recognizes the key and the degrees, checks ranges, spacing, voice crossings, doublings and note spelling, and explains every error.

It also describes each chord: its state, its melodic position and its spacing. That way you can check that you wrote exactly what you meant to write.

## What comes next

Now we know how to build a chord in four parts. Next we move the voices: [Lesson 7](/en/harmony-course/08-lesson-7-melodic-harmonic-motion), on **melodic and harmonic motion**, is already under construction. There we will see which intervals each voice may sing when moving from one chord to the next, and why we avoid parallel fifths and octaves.
