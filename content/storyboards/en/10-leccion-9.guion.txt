Before we move on to the minor mode, today we will learn how a musical phrase comes to an end: cadences.
This lesson has three parts: what a cadence is, the four types of cadence, and half cadences.
Let's begin.
We call a cadence a succession of chords with a conclusive intention.
In other words, a cadence tells us that the music has reached its end, or a point of rest.
Let's remember the three tonal regions: the subdominant, the dominant and the tonic.
The natural direction of a cadence leads us toward more and more important tonal regions.
That is why cadences usually go from the subdominant to the dominant, and from the dominant to the tonic.
And there is one rule for all of them: at least the last two chords of a cadence are in root position.
Root position gives the ending its firmness.
Now let's look at the four types of cadence.
The first is the authentic cadence: from the fifth degree to the first.
It is the clearest ending in tonal music, because the dominant resolves directly to the tonic.
Let's listen to an authentic cadence in G major.
The second is the plagal cadence: from the fourth degree to the first.
Here we do not pass through the dominant: we go from the subdominant straight to the tonic.
Its color is softer, which is why it is associated with the amen at the end of hymns.
Let's listen to a plagal cadence in B flat major.
The third is the complete cadence: it travels through all three regions, subdominant, dominant and tonic.
It begins with a chord from the subdominant region: the fourth degree, the second degree or the sixth degree.
The fourth and the second can be in root position or in six three.
Then comes the fifth degree, and finally the first, both in root position.
Let's listen to a complete cadence in D major, with the second degree in six three.
The fourth is the extended cadence.
It also begins in the subdominant region, with the fourth or the second degree, in root position or in six three.
But before the fifth degree a new chord appears: the first degree in second inversion, the six four.
In lesson eight we said that the six four is not used freely, only in specific cases.
This is the first of those cases.
The first degree in six four has the same bass note as the fifth degree.
That is why it does not sound like a tonic, but like an ornament that delays the arrival of the dominant.
And the most important rule: the first degree in six four of the extended cadence is placed on a strong beat.
In two four time, it goes on the first beat.
In three four time, also on the first beat.
And in four four time, on the first or the third beat.
The six four can last more than one beat, and the fifth degree comes afterwards, always before the final first degree.
Medrano points out only one exception: in three four time, the six four can go on the second beat, with the fifth on the third, right before the final first degree.
Let's listen to an extended cadence in E flat major.
From the fourth degree we move to the first in six four, on a strong beat, then to the fifth, and we end on the first.
Let's sum up the four types.
The authentic cadence goes from the fifth to the first.
The plagal cadence goes from the fourth to the first.
The complete cadence goes from the fourth, the second or the sixth, to the fifth and the first.
And the extended cadence goes from the fourth or the second, to the first in six four, to the fifth and the first.
Now let's look at half cadences.
We call a half cadence any partial rest inside a chorale.
These pauses are as important as breathing is for the choir.
They usually coincide with the end of a phrase of the text, which in a chorale is almost always religious.
After a half cadence we do not apply connection technique: the music starts again.
The most common half cadence is on the dominant.
We reach the fifth degree from the first, the second, the fourth or the sixth, and we stop there.
We can also reach the fifth through the first degree in six four, coming from the second or the fourth.
The second is the half cadence on the subdominant: from the first degree to the fourth.
And the third is the half cadence on the sixth degree, coming from the third, the fifth or the seventh in six three.
It is often called a deceptive cadence, but that name is not entirely appropriate.
It is much more precise to say: half cadence on the sixth degree.
And exceptionally, Bach writes a half cadence on the first degree in some of his chorales.
