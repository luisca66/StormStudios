Let's begin with the harmonic material.
We call harmonic material the chords we use, and the position in which we use them.
We follow Bach's chorales, and for now we work only with triads in the major mode.
The first degree is used in root position and in first inversion, that is, six three.
The second degree, also in root position and in six three.
The third degree is used only in root position.
The fourth and fifth degrees are used in root position and in six three.
The sixth degree, only in root position.
And the seventh degree, only in six three.
There are three things worth understanding about this list.
First, no chord is used freely in second inversion, six four.
In six four, a fourth forms above the bass, and that fourth sounds like a dissonance.
We will see six four chords later, in specific cases.
Second, the third and sixth degrees are used in root position.
These chords belong to two tonal regions and have a modal color, and root position gives them stability.
Third, the seventh degree is a diminished chord, so it is used only in six three.
That way we avoid a diminished fifth above the bass.
Now let's look at the general table of connections.
At least in the classical period, each chord has preferred paths toward other chords.
The table tells us which chords each one can go to.
When we say a chord is in root position or six three, either position is valid.
Let's start with the first degree.
From the first degree we can go to the second, the third, the fourth, the fifth, the sixth, and the seventh in six three.
But be careful: if we go from the first to the third, the third must then go to the fourth.

When the table says that after a connection we must go on to a certain chord, it is not a suggestion: it is a rule.
One more detail: when we repeat the same chord, there is no connection technique.

Now let's see how two chords are connected.
First we build each chord as in lesson six.
We respect the ranges and the maximum spacing between voices.
We use only the notes of the chord, always include the third, never double the leading tone, and the seventh degree is always complete.
Then we check how each voice moves, as in lesson seven.
Each voice uses only the allowed melodic intervals.
But now there is something new: a voice may stay on the same note.
When two chords share a note, we call it a common tone.
It is often best to keep the common tone in the same voice, and move the other voices by the shortest path.
Then we check the six pairs of voices.
Soprano and alto, soprano and tenor, soprano and bass, alto and tenor, alto and bass, and tenor and bass.
No pair may have parallel fifths or octaves, or contrary fifths or octaves.
Remember that contrary fifths are allowed when they are not between the soprano and the bass.
And two simultaneous leaps in the same direction are not allowed, unless both are perfect fourths.
And there is a new rule for the leading tone.
If the leading tone is in the soprano, and the next chord contains the tonic, the leading tone always rises to the tonic.
The only exception is the connection from the third to the fourth degree.
