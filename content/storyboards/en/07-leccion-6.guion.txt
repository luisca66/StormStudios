The first thing we need to know is the range of each voice.
Ranges vary a lot depending on the style, the composer and the choir, but in this course we will use fixed ranges.
We take C four as middle C on the piano.
The soprano sings from C four up to G five.
The alto, from G three up to D five.
The tenor, from B two up to G four.
And the bass, from F two up to B three.
The notes you write for each voice must stay within these limits.
The second thing is the spacing between the voices.
Between soprano and alto we use no more than a twelfth.
Between alto and tenor, no more than a tenth.
And between tenor and bass, no more than a fifteenth, that is, two octaves.
We want the three upper voices as compact as possible.
On the other hand, the further the bass is from the rest, the more robust the chord sounds.
The low note generates more overtones, and the other chord tones settle on them.
Also, the voices do not cross.
The soprano always stays above the alto, the alto above the tenor, and the tenor above the bass.
Now, a fifth chord has three notes: root, third and fifth.
But the quartet has four voices.
What do we do with three notes to make a four note chord?
We double one of them.
Let's look at a C major chord.
In the bass we write C three.
In the tenor, G three.
In the alto, E four.
And in the soprano, C again, on C five.
We are doubling the root.
Between bass and tenor there is a fifth, between tenor and alto a sixth, and between alto and soprano another sixth.
The ranges and the spacing are correct.
Let's listen to it.
We can double any note of the chord, with one exception: the leading tone.
The leading tone is the only note we never double.
In the major scale, the leading tone is the seventh degree.
In C major it is B.
The note B appears in three chords of C major: it is the third of the fifth degree, the root of the seventh and the fifth of the third.
In those three chords, B goes in a single voice.
The leading tone tends to resolve to the tonic; if we double it, that resolution ends up in two voices at once.
In major and minor chords, we preferably double the root or the fifth.
Doubling the third is possible, but it sounds a little harsh.
In major and minor chords we can also omit the fifth.
Then we double the root and the third.
Or we triple the root and use a single third.
Less often, we can triple the third.
What we never omit is the root or the third.
Without the root, the chord cannot be recognized.
And without the third, we cannot tell whether the chord is major or minor.
Diminished and augmented chords are always complete.
In C major, the diminished chord is the one on the seventh degree: B, D and F.
Its root, B, is the leading tone, so we double D or F.
The augmented chord appears in the harmonic minor scale, on the third degree.
In A harmonic minor it is C, E and G sharp.
Its fifth, G sharp, is the leading tone, so we double C or E.
Let's move on to the states of the chord.
The state depends on the note in the bass.
If the bass has the root, the chord is in root position.
If the bass has the third, it is in first inversion.
And if the bass has the fifth, it is in second inversion.
We figure first inversion as six three, and second inversion as six four.
These figures come from the intervals the other chord tones form above the bass.
Let's see an example with A major in first inversion.
A major is A, C sharp and E.
To put it in first inversion, we write C sharp in the bass.
We cannot omit the root, so we write A.
And here we double the root.
Now G flat major in second inversion.
G flat major is G flat, B flat and D flat.
For second inversion we need D flat in the bass.
We need the root and the third.
And now we double the fifth.
Let's listen to the three states.
Now the melodic position.
The melodic position depends on the note the soprano sings.
If the soprano has the root, we say the chord is in melodic position of the octave.
If it has the third, in melodic position of the third.
And if it has the fifth, in melodic position of the fifth.
Let's listen to the three positions.
Finally, the internal spacing.
Here we only look at the three upper voices: soprano, alto and tenor.
The spacing is close when no other note of the same chord fits between them.
And it is open when one does fit.
In this C major chord, between tenor and alto, and between alto and soprano, no other note of C major fits.
The spacing is close.
In this other one, a C fits between tenor and alto.
The spacing is open.
Let's listen to the difference.
