In the previous lesson we learned how to build chords for the vocal quartet.
Today we are going to see how the voices move when we go from one chord to the next.
This lesson has two parts: melodic motion and harmonic motion.
Melodic motion is about a single voice, and the intervals it sings from one note to the next.
Harmonic motion is about two voices moving at the same time.
When we connect two chords, each voice sings its own melody.
That is why, before we write our first chorale, we are going to practice each voice on its own.
I remember my first chorale very well.
There are so many things to watch at the same time that it is very easy to get lost.
So let's go step by step.
Let's start with melodic motion.
When one chord connects to another, each voice can only use these intervals.
The minor second and the major second.
The minor third and the major third.
The perfect fourth and the perfect fifth.
The minor sixth and the major sixth.
And the perfect octave.
There are no augmented intervals in this list.
There are no sevenths either, and nothing larger than an octave.
These intervals are classified into four groups.
Seconds are called steps.
Thirds are called short paths.
The perfect fourth is a short leap.
And the perfect fifth, the sixths and the octave are long leaps.
Let's listen to each one.
The step is the most natural motion for a voice.
Leaps give the melody character, but they have to be used with care.
The first rule of care has to do with the leading tone.
In C major, the leading tone is B, the note that wants to rise to C.
The leading tone never leaps an octave.
Now let's look at diminished intervals.
We said that augmented intervals are not used.
But the diminished fifth, the diminished fourth and the diminished seventh are allowed, on one condition.
After the diminished interval, the voice has to change direction.
We call this compensating.
In C major, from B up to F there is a diminished fifth.
If the voice then moves down, for example to E, the diminished fifth is compensated.
From F down to B there is also a diminished fifth.
If the voice then moves up, for example to C, it is also compensated.
But if after B and F the voice keeps rising, to G, the diminished fifth is left uncompensated.
And watch the direction.
From F up to B there is no diminished fifth, but an augmented fourth.
And augmented fourths are not used.
The diminished fourth and the diminished seventh appear in the minor mode, and we will see them later.
The last melodic rule is about successive leaps.
Two leaps in a row in the same direction are not used.
The exception is a fourth followed by a fifth, or a fifth followed by a fourth.
For example, from C to F and then to C, going up, is fine.
From C to G and then to C, too.
But from D to G and then to C are two fourths going up, and that is not used.
If the voice changes direction after a leap, there is no problem.
Now let's move on to harmonic motion.
Here we no longer look at a single voice, but at two voices moving at the same time.
The first rule is the most famous one in all of harmony.
Parallel fifths and parallel octaves are not used.
There are parallel fifths or octaves when two voices form a fifth or an octave, and then, moving in the same direction, form a fifth or an octave again.
For example, the soprano goes from C five to D five, and the bass goes from C three to D three.
The two voices are an octave apart, and they stay an octave apart.
Let's listen.
With parallel octaves, the two voices sound as if they were only one.
We lose a voice of the quartet.
Something similar happens with parallel fifths.
The perfect fifth is such a stable interval that the two voices blend and the sound becomes hollow.
Contrary fifths and contrary octaves are not used either.
They are the same fifths or octaves, but with the voices moving in opposite directions.
For example, the soprano rises from G four to C five while the bass falls from G three to C three.
We go from one octave to two octaves, and the effect is the same.
The unison also counts as an octave.
Now, there are two cases in which fifths are allowed.
The first is parallel fifths when one of the two, or both, are not perfect.
For example, from a perfect fifth to a diminished fifth.
Or from a diminished fifth to a perfect fifth.
What is not allowed is going from one perfect fifth to another perfect fifth.
The second case is contrary fifths in voices that are not the outer voices.
The outer voices are the soprano and the bass, the top one and the bottom one.
Between the soprano and the bass, contrary fifths are not used.
But between the tenor and the bass, for example, they are allowed.
The last harmonic rule is about simultaneous leaps.
We avoid two voices leaping at the same time in the same direction.
The exception is when both leaps are perfect fourths.
If two voices leap at the same time but in opposite directions, there is no problem.
Let's listen to a connection of two chords with parallel fifths.
